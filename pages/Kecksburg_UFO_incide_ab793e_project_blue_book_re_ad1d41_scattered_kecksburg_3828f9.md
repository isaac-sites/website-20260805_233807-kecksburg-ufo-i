## Introduction

One reason the documentary record surrounding the 1965 Kecksburg incident appears incomplete is that researchers often assume every relevant document would have been filed under the place name "Kecksburg" or inside a single [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) case file. In practice, US government record systems of the period were fragmented. A widely observed fireball crossing several US states and parts of Canada could generate reports in multiple commands, agencies and filing systems, each using different indexing rules. That means the absence of extensive material in a Kecksburg-labelled folder does not necessarily imply that no additional records were ever created. It does, however, mean that proving the existence of further records requires evidence from multiple archival collections rather than relying on a single [Blue Book file]({{ 'blue-book-file/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/textual-and-microfilm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

## Why a place-name search may miss relevant records

The Kecksburg incident was not experienced as a purely local event. Witnesses across Ontario and several US states reported a bright fireball moving across the sky before attention focused on the wooded area near Kecksburg, Pennsylvania. Different organisations therefore encountered different parts of the event.

From an administrative perspective, records might have been indexed according to:

For the Kecksburg incident, this distinction is especially important because different questions would have gone to different organisations. A radar observation, a press enquiry, an intelligence assessment and a Blue Book classification were not necessarily documented in the same administrative stream. Even if all concerned the same evening, their records might never have been physically combined.

## Regional reporting beyond the name "Kecksburg"

The geography of the 9 December 1965 fireball provides another mechanism for dispersed filing.

Witnesses first reported the luminous object hundreds of kilometres before it reached western Pennsylvania. Military personnel, police agencies, observatories and civil authorities receiving reports earlier along the flight path may have recorded observations under their own jurisdictions rather than under the eventual location where local residents believed an object descended.

Examples of alternative filing approaches include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* state-based incident logs rather than town names;
* reports organised by Air Defense Command sectors;
* radar-unit daily logs;
* chronological message traffic for 9–10 December 1965;
* correspondence generated by media enquiries;
* investigations into possible satellite re-entry.

</div>

A researcher searching exclusively for "Kecksburg" therefore risks overlooking documentation whose authors had no reason to use that place name when creating the original record.

## Why archival catalogues can complicate the search

Modern archival systems preserve the administrative structure under which records were originally created. The National Archives organises UAP-related holdings by record group, series and file unit rather than by producing a single master file for every historical incident. ## What scattered filing can—and cannot—explain

The scattered-filing hypothesis provides a plausible administrative explanation for why the surviving Project Blue Book material appears relatively brief. Government records from complex, multi-agency events are often distributed across numerous filing systems, and later archivists generally preserve that original separation.

However, this mechanism has clear limits. It explains how relevant records might become difficult to locate, but it does not establish that extensive undiscovered Kecksburg files actually exist. Likewise, dispersed filing should not be confused with evidence of suppression. Demonstrating that additional records survive requires identifying actual documents in other collections, not merely showing that alternative filing paths were possible.

Within the broader discussion of the Project Blue Book record gap, scattered filing is therefore best understood as a reasonable archival mechanism that complicates historical research, rather than as proof either for or against claims of a recovered object or an official cover-up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/textual-and-microfilm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>
