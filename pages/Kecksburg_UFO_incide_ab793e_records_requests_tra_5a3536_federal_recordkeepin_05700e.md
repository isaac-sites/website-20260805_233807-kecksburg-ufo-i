## Introduction

The missing or incomplete federal records associated with the 1965 Kecksburg incident are often presented as evidence of secrecy. A more cautious reading is that they also illustrate a longstanding challenge in US government recordkeeping. Modern federal law requires agencies to preserve official records, document their destruction through approved schedules, and report any unlawful loss to the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA). However, those safeguards work only if records are correctly identified, managed and still exist when questions arise. Once files have been lawfully destroyed under an approved retention schedule, accidentally lost, misfiled or never properly indexed, neither the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) nor later archival reviews can reliably reconstruct them. This distinction is central to understanding why decades-old gaps in the Kecksburg documentary record remain unresolved.

## Federal duties to preserve official records

Federal agencies are governed principally by the Federal Records Act, which requires them to create and maintain records documenting their activities and to manage those records according to schedules approved by the Archivist of the United States. Records may not simply be discarded at an employee's discretion; destruction is permitted only under NARA-approved records schedules or applicable General Records Schedules. Agencies are also expected to train personnel, establish internal controls and prevent the unlawful or accidental destruction or removal of records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/36/1230.10" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic Code of Federal Regulations (e-CFR) &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

These obligations matter when considering historical cases such as Kecksburg because they distinguish between several very different possibilities:

FOIA can compel agencies to search for existing records, but it cannot require production of records that no longer exist or were never created. That legal limitation became especially relevant during later litigation over NASA's searches relating to the Kecksburg incident.

## How unauthorised destruction is supposed to be reported

Current federal regulations impose a clear reporting process when records are believed to have been unlawfully or accidentally destroyed.

Under 44 U.S.C. §3106 and implementing regulations in 36 CFR Part 1230, an agency head must notify the Archivist of any actual, impending or threatened unlawful removal, alteration or destruction of records. The agency's report must describe what records were affected, explain the circumstances, identify measures to prevent further loss and, where possible, outline efforts to recover or reconstruct the missing documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title44-section3106" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[house.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 23, 2026</span></span></span>

NARA then reviews the allegation and requests information from the agency. If records appear to have been unlawfully removed and the agency fails to pursue recovery, the Archivist has authority to involve the Attorney General and notify Congress where appropriate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/36/1230.16" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic Code of Federal Regulations (e-CFR) &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

These procedures are designed primarily to stop losses while they are occurring or shortly afterwards. They are far less effective when questions emerge many decades later, after staff have retired, offices have reorganised and paper files have passed through multiple storage systems.

## Why Kecksburg falls into a difficult historical period

The Kecksburg incident occurred in December 1965, during an era when federal records were overwhelmingly paper-based and often managed independently by individual offices before eventual transfer to federal records centres or the National Archives.

Several characteristics of recordkeeping at the time complicate retrospective investigation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* filing systems frequently depended on office-specific subject classifications rather than modern searchable databases;
* records relating to operations involving multiple agencies could be divided among separate filing systems;
* temporary operational files often had shorter authorised retention periods than policy records;
* documentation of routine administrative actions was sometimes considered disposable after a specified number of years.

</div>

Consequently, even where agencies complied with the rules in force at the time, later investigators may encounter incomplete documentary trails simply because temporary files were never intended for permanent preservation.

This does not prove that every [missing Kecksburg]({{ 'missing-boxes/' | relative_url }})-related document was lawfully destroyed. Rather, it explains why an incomplete archival record is compatible with ordinary federal records management practices as well as with more controversial interpretations.

## What the NASA litigation actually revealed

The FOIA litigation brought by journalist Leslie Kean demonstrated weaknesses in NASA's search process rather than proving that records had been intentionally destroyed.

During the case, NASA acknowledged that its initial searches had been inadequate, leading to a court-supervised settlement requiring broader searches across additional offices and repositories. The expanded review ultimately produced hundreds of additional pages concerning historical debris recovery activities and related subjects, but no definitive document establishing that NASA had recovered an unidentified craft at Kecksburg. At the same time, some categories of expected historical material could not be located, leaving unresolved questions about whether certain records had been lost, destroyed under approved schedules or never existed in the anticipated form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/resources/unauthorized-dispositions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Unauthorized Dispositions &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUnauthorized Dispositions &#124; National ArchivesDecember 8, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 8, 2017</span></span></span>

From a recordkeeping perspective, this illustrates an important distinction:

* inadequate searches can be corrected through litigation;
* destroyed or missing records generally cannot be recreated through litigation.

A successful FOIA lawsuit therefore improves the search for surviving documents but cannot reverse historical record losses.

## What modern recordkeeping rules cannot recover decades later

Even today's stronger preservation framework has practical limits when applied to events from the 1960s.

### Records must first be recognised as records

Preservation rules apply only to material identified as federal records. Working notes, duplicate copies, routine administrative papers and other categories may historically have been scheduled differently from permanent investigative files. If offices classified material inconsistently, later researchers may discover uneven survival across related collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/about/laws/fed-agencies.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

### Reporting requirements are not retroactive investigations

Current requirements to report unlawful destruction are intended to address ongoing or recently discovered losses. They are not designed to reconstruct events after half a century has passed, particularly where there is no surviving evidence establishing when a document disappeared.

### Reconstruction has practical limits

Regulations encourage agencies to salvage or reconstruct records where possible. In practice, reconstruction usually means locating duplicate copies, related correspondence or records held elsewhere—not recreating documents from memory decades after the original files have vanished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/36/1230.14" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Legal Information Institute36 CFR § 1230.14 - How do agencies report incidents? &#124; Electronic Code of Federal Regulations (e-CFR) &#124; US Law...</span></span></span>

## Could better recordkeeping have prevented the gaps?

Modern records management practices would probably have reduced some of the uncertainty surrounding Kecksburg, although they could not have guaranteed complete documentation.

Compared with the systems available in 1965, today's federal environment generally includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* centralised electronic records management;(#endnote-1 "Endnote 1")<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=21-8o7AV560" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: youtube.com">[youtube.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">youtube.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI - Records Management Overview...</span></span></span>
* standardised records schedules across agencies;
* stronger oversight by agency records officers;
* clearer reporting requirements for unauthorised destruction;
* improved audit trails for electronic records.

</div>

These developments increase the likelihood that records can later be located and that [missing files]({{ 'missing-files/' | relative_url }}) leave an identifiable administrative trail. They do not eliminate every possibility of loss, particularly in complex multi-agency events.

The Kecksburg case therefore illustrates a broader lesson about government transparency. Missing historical records should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of concealment, but neither should they be dismissed as unimportant administrative errors. The Federal Records Act and related regulations have progressively strengthened preservation and accountability, yet those protections depend on records being correctly created, identified and retained before they disappear. Once a documentary gap has existed for decades, modern transparency laws can expose the gap more clearly than they can fill it.

    }

        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
